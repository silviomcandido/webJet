4.2. Three-Dimensional Instabilities

Electrohydrodynamic (EHD) jets can operate in a wide range of regimes, from stable axisymmetric cone-jets to strongly unsteady bending and whipping motions. These behaviours result from the competition between electric stresses, surface tension, inertia and viscosity.

At moderate electric forcing, the liquid meniscus and the emitted jet remain approximately aligned with the capillary axis. As the electric potential increases, small lateral perturbations may grow, causing the jet centreline to move away from the axis. The jet can then develop oscillatory bending and, under sufficiently strong forcing, a three-dimensional whipping trajectory.

These instabilities cannot be reproduced with a two-dimensional axisymmetric simulation. Axisymmetry constrains the jet centreline to remain on the nozzle axis and suppresses all azimuthal disturbances. A fully three-dimensional calculation is therefore required to predict the direction, amplitude and temporal evolution of the lateral motion.

This section investigates the transition from an approximately axisymmetric cone-jet to non-axisymmetric oscillations using the numerical methodology described in the Modelling chapter. The analysis focuses on the effect of the electric capillary number, CaE, and on the physical mechanisms responsible for the onset of whipping.

For the geometry, fluid properties, flow conditions and electrical boundary conditions considered here, sustained three-dimensional motion is observed for CaE greater than approximately 0.25. This value should be interpreted as specific to the present configuration rather than as a universal instability threshold.

4.2.1. Problem Definition

The computational configuration consists of a metallic capillary facing a grounded collector. The capillary has an internal diameter Di = 160 µm, an external diameter Do = 260 µm and a modelled length L = 300 µm.

The capillary tip is positioned at a distance H = 1.5 mm from the collector. The collector has a diameter Dc = 5 mm, sufficiently larger than the characteristic jet diameter to minimise the influence of its lateral edge on the jet dynamics.

Liquid is supplied through the capillary using a prescribed inlet condition, while the surrounding gas is initially at rest. The capillary is maintained at an electric potential Φ and the collector is grounded. The resulting electric field deforms the liquid interface, forms the Taylor cone and accelerates the emitted jet towards the collector.

The lateral dimensions of the domain are selected to reduce the influence of the outer boundaries on the electric field and on the gas-phase flow surrounding the jet.

Because the modelled capillary length is relatively short, the inlet profile is prescribed consistently with the internal capillary flow rather than assuming that a uniform profile becomes fully developed within the simulated length.

4.2.2. Effect of the Electric Potential

The effect of the electric forcing is characterised by the electric capillary number, CaE, which compares electric stresses with the restoring stresses associated with surface tension.

At low values of CaE, capillary forces are sufficiently strong to suppress lateral disturbances. The meniscus and the emitted jet therefore remain approximately axisymmetric, apart from small transient oscillations.

As CaE increases, the electric stresses acting on the liquid interface become stronger. The meniscus is further elongated and the emitted jet becomes more sensitive to small asymmetric perturbations.

Above the stability limit, the jet centreline begins to oscillate laterally. The displacement grows with time and the jet evolves towards a fully three-dimensional bending or whipping motion. The direction of this displacement is not prescribed, but develops naturally from the numerical solution.

Figure 2 shows successive liquid-interface iso-surfaces over a total interval of 1.5 ms. When the surfaces remain closely grouped, the jet is stable or only weakly oscillatory. A wider trajectory envelope indicates the growth of non-axisymmetric motion.

4.2.3. Physical Interpretation

The instability results from the competition between destabilising electric stresses and restoring capillary and viscous stresses. Surface tension tends to maintain a straight and compact jet by reducing the liquid-gas interfacial area, while viscosity dissipates the energy associated with lateral motion.

When the jet is displaced laterally, the electric field around it becomes asymmetric. This asymmetry produces an unbalanced electric traction on the liquid interface. If the electric forcing is sufficiently strong, the resulting traction amplifies the initial displacement rather than restoring the jet towards the axis.

This creates a feedback mechanism: a small lateral displacement modifies the electric field, the modified field produces an asymmetric electric stress, and that stress generates a further displacement.

The three-dimensional formulation is essential because the orientation of the lateral motion is not known in advance. An axisymmetric model only admits the axisymmetric mode and therefore cannot reproduce bending or whipping, regardless of the magnitude of the applied voltage.

4.2.4. Characterisation of the Instability

The onset of instability can be assessed by tracking the jet centreline as a function of time. Relevant quantities include the maximum radial displacement, oscillation amplitude, dominant frequency and temporal growth rate.

These quantities provide a more objective criterion than visual inspection alone. A stable jet presents a bounded and weak centreline displacement, while an unstable jet exhibits a growing lateral amplitude or a sustained three-dimensional trajectory.

The three-dimensional results are first compared with the corresponding axisymmetric simulations in the stable regime. The model is then assessed against published experimental observations of Taylor cone-jets, including the cone shape, jet behaviour and transition to lateral motion.

4.2.5. Numerical Reliability

The predicted instability threshold is sensitive to the spatial and temporal resolution. The mesh must resolve the thin emitted jet, the interface curvature and the strong electric-field gradients near the capillary tip.

Insufficient resolution may artificially damp the instability or alter the jet diameter. Conversely, numerical disturbances associated with the interface reconstruction may act as an initial perturbation and trigger lateral motion.

The critical value of CaE should therefore be interpreted together with mesh- and time-step-sensitivity studies. Suitable convergence indicators include the mean jet diameter, cone shape, maximum centreline displacement and oscillation frequency.

Within these numerical limitations, the three-dimensional model captures physical behaviour that is fundamentally inaccessible to an axisymmetric formulation and provides a direct description of the transition from a stable cone-jet to bending and whipping.
